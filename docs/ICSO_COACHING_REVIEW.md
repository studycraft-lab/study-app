# Grade 6 ICSO coaching review

Reviewed 7 October 2026 and updated after adding two more chapters. This is a product and learning-design audit of the seven local ICSO lessons available on that date, not evidence that these lessons improve a child's exam score. The stated 27–30 minute durations are estimates; no child timing or learning-outcome study has been run.

On 8 October 2026, History & Generations of Computers and Windows 11 were added, bringing the ICSO collection to nine lessons. Their source and accuracy checks are recorded in [History lesson sources](HISTORY_ICSO_LESSON_SOURCES.md) and [Windows 11 lesson sources](WINDOWS_11_ICSO_LESSON_SOURCES.md). The recommendations below still apply to all nine lessons.

## Verdict

The short mission → practice desk → quick check → final challenge format is a sound starting point. The Word, PowerPoint, and HTML/CSS/Scratch lessons let the child operate simplified interfaces. Computer Fundamentals and Memory/Storage use sorting workbenches and small power/access simulations. Networking/Cyber Safety and AI/Robotics use original scenarios and diagrams, but most of their practice is recognition from multiple-choice options. None of the seven yet provides a robust coaching loop across days. Therefore it would be inaccurate to call the current experience state of the art or a complete Olympiad preparation system.

| Dimension | Current state | What to build next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Active practice | Word, PowerPoint and HTML/CSS/Scratch have clickable practice desks. Fundamentals and Memory/Storage sort cases into roles, with small power/access models. Networking and AI mostly use choices; network scope and topology diagrams are directly clickable. | Add one short task that produces or diagnoses an artifact per concept chapter, such as choosing a safe response to a simulated message or tracing a packet route. |
| Feedback | Each chapter explains final answers. The four concept chapters distinguish first-attempt checks from guided practice and list topics to revisit. Networking/AI give targeted feedback on seven high-risk misconceptions; Fundamentals/Memory give a mix of targeted and general feedback. | Extend error-specific feedback to every plausible misconception and make the revisit link directly to a focused retry. |
| Retrieval | Each chapter has an immediate quick check and final challenge. | Save a small record of first attempts, then bring back 2–3 mixed questions after a delay on a future visit. Avoid presenting an immediate corrected retry as proof of mastery. |
| Reasoning and transfer | Most questions ask for selection; some use scenarios and multiple statements similar in *form* to recent papers. | Ask for one brief explanation or prediction before showing choices. Include a new context that was not used in the model. Have a human review open responses until automatic feedback is validated. |
| Coverage and accuracy | Each chapter has a source note and accuracy audit. The four concept chapters were checked against saved synopses, recent downloaded papers, and topic documentation. | Audit the signed-in SOF chapter banks for the four concept chapters before claiming comprehensive exam alignment. Check all seven lessons against future syllabus and product-version changes. |
| Personalization and accessibility | Hints, keyboard-accessible buttons, responsive layouts, and local restart are present. | Trial with the child: measure completion time, confusion, reading load, keyboard use, and whether the lesson can be resumed. Adapt later practice from observed first attempts, rather than assuming a single score is mastery. |

## Evidence behind the priorities

- The [US What Works Clearinghouse practice guide](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/1) recommends spacing, alternating worked examples and problems, combining graphics with explanations, quizzing for re-exposure, and deep explanatory questions. Its evidence ratings differ by recommendation; the format alone is not an efficacy claim.
- The [Education Endowment Foundation feedback guidance](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/feedback) stresses addressing misunderstandings and the quality of feedback, rather than whether it is delivered in writing or video.
- The [Australian Education Research Organisation spacing and retrieval guide](https://www.edresearch.edu.au/guides-resources/practice-guides/spacing-and-retrieval-practice-guide-full-publication) recommends retrieval from memory in varied ways across lessons, prompt correction of errors, and low-stakes practice. It cautions that easy retrieval may be mistaken for mastery.

## Recommended 30-minute shape for the next revision

Use six independent 3–4 minute parts and a 5–6 minute mixed challenge. In each part: show one concise example, ask the child to predict or act, respond to the specific error, then ask a different question without showing the example. End with a two-minute explanation or application task by replacing some multiple-choice time, rather than adding time. On another day, offer a separate two-minute recall round outside this lesson's 30-minute cap. Validate the timing with the child before advertising a guaranteed duration.

Do not use video by default. A 30–60 second clip is useful when movement or timing is itself the concept; the child should still be able to pause, try an action, and receive feedback. The most important improvement is the quality and spacing of practice, not adding more media or AI features.
