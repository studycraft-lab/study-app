# Parent and student interview kit

## Purpose

Use this kit to discover recent behavior, observe the current revision job, and test commitment. It is not a sales script or a satisfaction survey. Interview the parent and student separately first; a child often gives the answer they think the parent wants when the parent is present.

Target 30-40 minutes for a parent, 20-30 minutes for a student, and 20-30 minutes for an observed usability session. Ask permission before recording. Do not collect more child identifiers than the beta needs.

## Evidence rules for interviewers

Label every note:

- **ARTIFACT:** textbook page, worksheet, timetable, messages, answer sheet, receipt, or other evidence shown.
- **OBSERVED:** interviewer saw the behavior during the session.
- **SELF-REPORT:** participant described behavior; useful but subject to recall and social desirability.
- **COMMITMENT:** participant gave time, data, referral, deposit, or payment.
- **HYPOTHESIS:** interviewer interpretation requiring another test.

Ask “Tell me about the last time…” before “Would you…”. Ask for a real choice before a 1-10 rating. Do not explain the product until the current behavior is understood.

Avoid:

- “Do you like this idea?”
- “Would this save you time?”
- “Would you pay INR 299 per month?” without a purchase choice;
- “Is subjective grading important?”
- “Don't you worry that ChatGPT hallucinates?”
- asking a child to criticize a parent while that parent is present; and
- treating compliments as demand.

## Consent and child assent

This is operational guidance, not legal advice. Use counsel-approved notices and consent flows before collecting child data or uploaded textbook pages.

### Parent opening

> We are studying how families prepare for school tests and trying a private chapter-practice service. I want your real experience, including things that did not work. This is not a school assessment. With your permission I will take notes [and record]. You can skip any question or stop. We will not ask your child for school login details or contact lists. May I continue?

Confirm separately:

- permission to interview the child;
- whether the parent may leave the room for the student section;
- permission for any audio/screen recording;
- permission and authority to provide the chapter pages;
- current source-retention choice and deletion expectation; and
- whether anonymized quotations may be used.

### Child assent opening

> I am testing the tool, not testing you. Some questions may be bad; that is our fault, and you can say so. You can skip anything or stop, and your school marks will not be affected. I will tell your parent about product problems, but I will not use your name in a report. Is it okay to continue? Is it okay if I [take notes/record the screen]?

If the child declines, stop even if the parent consented. Do not make an incentive depend on finishing or positive feedback.

# Parent interview guide

## A. Screener and household context — 5 minutes

1. What grade and board is each school-going child in? What language are their textbooks and school instruction?
2. Which Science and Social Studies books/publishers/editions are used? Are school notes or worksheets more important than the textbook for tests?
3. What devices can the child use for homework? Which are shared, and when are they available?
4. Who normally notices an upcoming test first? Who decides what the child studies?
5. Does anyone outside the household help—tuition teacher, coaching class, older sibling, online course? What was used in the last 30 days?

**Interviewer record:** grade, board, subject, publisher, device, parent role, current paid alternatives, relationship code. Do not infer income from school name.

## B. Reconstruct the most recent test — 10 minutes

1. Think of the child's most recent Science or Social Studies test. What date was it and which chapters were included?
2. When did preparation begin? Walk me through the sequence from the first study session to the test morning.
3. Please show me what you used: chapter, notes, worksheet, guidebook, messages, schedule, or answer sheet.
4. What did your child do alone? What did you do? What did a tutor do?
5. How did you decide the child was “ready”? What evidence did you have?
6. Tell me one question the child could explain orally but struggled to write—or the reverse.
7. What did the child get wrong on the actual test? When did you discover it?
8. How many active minutes did you spend reading, making questions, asking, checking, reminding, or correcting? Break it down rather than giving one total.
9. What did you choose not to do because time ran out?
10. What was frustrating enough that you tried to change it?

**Probes:** “What happened next?” “Who did that?” “Show me.” “How often in the last four tests?” “What makes that example typical or unusual?”

## C. Current alternatives and money — 7 minutes

1. What did you most recently pay for that helped with school subjects—not competitive exams? Ask for the exact item, month, and amount; request a receipt only if comfortable.
2. Which guidebook, worksheet pack, app, or tuition class was used most recently? What happened after purchase?
3. Which paid resource was abandoned? When and why?
4. Have you used ChatGPT, Gemini, NotebookLM, Quizlet, Brainly, or another AI tool with your child's schoolwork? Show the last use if possible. Who operated it? Did anyone verify the answer?
5. When an answer key and your child disagree, whose judgment do you trust and what do you do?
6. If you had to remove one current support—your own questioning, guidebooks, tuition, or apps—which would be hardest to lose? Why?
7. What has to happen before you pay for a new education product? Whose approval is needed?

Do not ask general “willingness to pay” yet. Establish the existing budget, switching threshold, and abandoned purchases first.

## D. How the parent role is changing — 5 minutes

1. Compare this year with two grades ago. What schoolwork do you still supervise that you did not expect to?
2. What has the child started doing independently?
3. What have you stopped checking? Why—trust, lack of time, subject difficulty, tuition, or the child resisting?
4. At what point do you expect the child to take over test planning? What recent behavior supports that expectation?
5. Who would operate a chapter-practice tool today? Who would operate it next year?

Code the household as **parent operator**, **shared operator**, **student operator**, or **delegated to tutor**. Do not code from grade alone.

## E. Upload, rights, privacy, and trust — 7 minutes

Show a neutral two-screen concept only after the behavior sections: (1) upload chapter pages, (2) reviewed bank with source citations and rubric points.

1. Hand me or point to the exact chapter you would choose today. Why that one?
2. Without help, show how you would send these pages from the device you normally use. Observe ordering, glare, missing pages, PDF access, and time.
3. What would stop you from uploading the pages? Do not offer answer options first.
4. Who owns or provided this copy? What would you expect the service to do with the images after making the bank?
5. Choose one default and explain: delete source images after extraction/review, or retain them privately for citations and later reuse. What information would change your choice?
6. What child information is acceptable to store? What feels unnecessary?
7. Which would reduce trust most: one wrong objective answer, one unfair subjective score, a missing topic, a privacy incident, or a slow bank? Force a ranking.
8. Show this sample rubric and score. What evidence would you need to accept or challenge it?
9. If an adult reviewer may see the chapter and generated questions, what would you need to know about that person and their access?

Record actual upload time and errors. A stated willingness to upload is weaker than completing an upload.

## F. Commitment and price — 5 minutes

Use this only after the family has seen or used a real output from its own chapter.

> The first reviewed chapter is free. The Founding Trial then costs INR 99 for one child and the next three chapters or 30 pages, valid for 30 days. It includes ordinary practice and subjective-answer feedback. Would you like to buy it today, continue with only the free chapter, or decline?

If “buy”:

- offer the real checkout/deposit flow;
- do not offer an unplanned discount;
- ask what budget this replaces or supplements; and
- record whether the child has another relevant chapter in the next 30 days.

If “not now”:

1. What specifically prevents the purchase today?
2. What will you do for the next test instead?
3. Which is the main reason: insufficient value, no upcoming need, child will not use it, trust, price, upload work, existing tuition/resource, privacy/rights, or something else?
4. May we contact you after the next test? A “yes” is not a payment.

After the INR 99 trial has been used, make a real second offer rather than asking only a hypothetical question:

- INR 199 for five additional chapters; or
- INR 299 for ten additional chapters/100 pages.

Record checkout and payment. If the parent declines, ask which current alternative they will use for the next test. Do not convert stated preference into revenue.

## G. Parent post-use interview — after first and second chapter

1. Show me exactly when the product was used and by whom.
2. Which session required you to intervene? What did you do?
3. How many minutes did you spend uploading, checking, prompting, and resolving problems?
4. Show one result you trusted and one you checked.
5. Which question or score was wrong, incomplete, or confusing? What would you have done without us?
6. Did the child return without being told? What triggered the return?
7. What did this replace: your questioning, a worksheet, a guidebook, tuition time, or nothing?
8. What happened in the school test? Ask for the answer sheet if available, while avoiding a marks-guarantee claim.
9. Why did you submit—or not submit—a second chapter?
10. If the service disappeared today, what would you do for the next test?

# Student interview guide

Interview without the parent answering for the child. Use simpler language for younger students and stop if the child is tired.

## A. Last real study session — 7 minutes

1. Tell me about the last Science or Social Studies test you studied for. What did you do first?
2. Show me the chapter or notes you actually used.
3. How did you know what was important?
4. Who asked you questions? What happened when you did not know an answer?
5. Did you write answers, say them aloud, reread, highlight, watch a video, or solve a worksheet? Put these in the order you did them.
6. What did you do when you were studying alone?
7. Which part made you want to stop?
8. Was there something you thought you knew but could not write in the test?

Avoid “Do your parents help you?” Ask for the last event, action, and sequence.

## B. Control and motivation — 5 minutes

1. Who usually tells you it is time to study? What happened yesterday or the last study day?
2. If nobody reminded you, when would you start before a test?
3. Which feels worse: too many easy questions, a few very hard questions, writing long answers, being told an answer is wrong without why, or repeating something you know? Rank them.
4. When you get an answer wrong, what kind of help makes you try again?
5. When do points, stars, streaks, or scores help? When do they feel annoying or babyish?
6. Would you rather stop after ten questions, after a section is mastered, or at a time limit? Why? Then observe actual stopping behavior later.

## C. Devices and response mode — 4 minutes

1. Show me the device you use for schoolwork. Is it yours or shared?
2. What was the last long answer you typed? Where and how long was it?
3. Have you sent a voice answer or photo of handwriting for school? Show the last example if allowed.
4. For this exact question, choose one: type, speak, photograph handwriting, write with a stylus, or answer aloud to a person. Why?
5. Now try the available typed mode. Record behavior before believing the preference.

The MVP decision should follow observed completion, device constraints, error rates, and privacy—not a popularity vote among modalities.

## D. Ten-question observed task — 10-15 minutes

Give the child a real reviewed session from the supplied chapter. Say: “Please work as you normally would. You can stop at any time. Tell me what you are thinking only if that does not make it harder.”

Record without coaching:

- time to understand the first action;
- whether the child seeks the textbook, parent, hint, or answer;
- answer-entry errors, scrolling, keyboard problems, and device orientation;
- completion by question type;
- reading versus writing time;
- reaction to correct/incorrect/partial feedback;
- whether the rubric language is understood;
- signs of embarrassment, anxiety, gaming, random guessing, or fatigue;
- whether the child starts the next question/session voluntarily; and
- requests to stop.

Afterward ask:

1. Which question was unfair or confusing? Show me why.
2. Pick one score you disagree with. What should it have been?
3. Without looking, tell me one thing the feedback helped you correct.
4. Which question would help for the school test? Which would not?
5. If you had 15 minutes tomorrow, would you choose another session, the textbook, a worksheet, or something else? Why?
6. May I ask you tomorrow what you actually chose?

## E. Student post-use interview — 3-14 days later

1. Since we last spoke, when did you open the practice again? Show the history if comfortable.
2. Who reminded you each time?
3. What did you do instead on days you did not use it?
4. Which correction can you still remember?
5. Did you change an answer in school or homework because of it?
6. Which type of question did you skip or rush? Why?
7. If your parent bought it, would that make you more likely to use it, less likely, or no difference? What happened with the last app/book they bought?
8. What should be removed before anything is added?

# Interviewer scorecards

## Parent scorecard

Score 0, 1, or 2 from evidence, not enthusiasm.

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current pain | No recent active checking | Occasional/low-cost workaround | Repeated time or cash burden shown |
| Upcoming trigger | None | Vague future need | Real chapter/test within 30 days |
| Upload behavior | Refuses/cannot | Completes with heavy help | Completes independently in acceptable time |
| Trust | Rejects AI/adult review | Conditional with major concerns | Accepts transparent rubric/citation workflow after checking |
| Child activation | No completed session | One prompted session | Three sessions, at least one without direct prompting |
| Commitment | Compliment/contact only | Deposit or scheduled action | Full real payment |
| Repeat | No second chapter | Intent/scheduled chapter | Second distinct chapter used |

A score is for cohort comparison, not an automated sales decision. Record disqualifying privacy/safety concerns separately.

## Student scorecard

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Independent operation | Cannot proceed | Occasional help | Completes without help |
| Session completion | Under 40% | 40-79% | 80%+ |
| Feedback comprehension | Cannot explain | Understands some | Can explain score/correction |
| Fatigue/affect | Distressed or refuses | Mixed/tolerates | Engaged without pressure |
| Voluntary return | No | Prompted only | Returns/chooses next session |
| Written-answer fit | Input prevents valid response | Some friction | Can express answer adequately |
