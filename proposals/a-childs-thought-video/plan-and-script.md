# A Child’s Thought
## Approved video plan and tutor script

**Poet:** Robert Louis Stevenson  
**Learner:** Class 6, ICSE  
**Version:** 1 — approved script; full video produced 27 September 2026  
**Video length:** 8:54, including thinking pauses  
**Format:** The same expressive American Runway tutor voice used in the finished *A Little Grain of Gold* lesson, highlighted poem text, original animated illustrations, and captions. No on-screen tutor avatar.

### Teaching approach

Read all twenty lines in six short passages, preserving the two stanzas. After each passage, explain the words, picture the scene, and connect it to the child’s feelings. The main thread is the contrast between vivid bedtime imagination and the ordinary room on waking.

By the end, the learner should be able to describe the dream world, match its features to the waking-room objects, explain the change of mood, identify personification in “the castle frowned,” and support an answer with a short quotation.

| Section | Approximate time | Visual treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Opening and introduction | 40 seconds | A bedroom at bedtime; a clock showing seven; a window into imagination |
| Six read-and-explain passages | 5–6 minutes | Highlighted lines, castles, dragons, gardens, riders, streams, and the return to the bedroom |
| Contrast and poetic choices | 1½–2 minutes | Paired dream/room images; personification, rhyme, repetition, and stanza structure |
| Understanding check and closing | 1–1½ minutes | Three spoken questions with pauses and answers; a concise recap |

Final timings will follow the recorded narration. The video will have eleven navigable sections in StudyCraft.

### Voice and screen direction

- Use the selected Runway voice throughout. The opening is curious and inviting; the dream passages carry wonder and a little adventure; the waking passages become quieter and wistful. Avoid a flat lecture delivery or exaggerated character voices.
- Read the verse a little more slowly than the explanation. Follow the meaning through line breaks: “streams / That border…” and “ran / Is now…” should flow together. Give a fuller pause at the stanza break.
- Keep the printed line breaks visible. Where a screen needs more space, use successive cards without suggesting a new stanza. Highlight the spoken phrase, then highlight relevant words during explanation.
- Use large dark text on a cream background, with the same soft gold highlight as the previous lesson. Keep narration captions in a separate band below the poem.
- Establish one consistent bedroom. Dream scenes use the same broad positions as their waking counterparts, so the contrast is easy to follow. Use a labelled dissolve from **IN THE CHILD’S IMAGINATION** to **ON WAKING**; do not imply that real furniture physically changes by magic.
- Show original, gentle storybook illustrations: a castle with a watchful dragon, magical fruit, a tower, a shaded bower, brave riders, and rippling streams. Do not add a rescue, battle, or other event absent from the poem.
- For “the castle frowned,” pair the stern-looking castle with a small illustration of a human frown during the explanation. Do not present a literal moving face on the castle as an event in the poem.
- Six five-second thinking pauses are included below. The learner can pause for longer. These are spoken teaching pauses; the later StudyCraft feature that requires an objective answer before resuming is a separate cut.
- No background music under the teaching. Keep any opening or closing sound brief and unobtrusive.

## Scene 1 — A whole world inside your head

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Title and poet. A child settles into bed. A clock points to seven; the window shows evening. A faint outline of an imaginary castle appears, without revealing the morning comparison yet.

**SAY:**

“Have you ever imagined a whole adventure while lying in bed? Perhaps your ordinary room seemed to disappear, and a completely different world took its place.

“In ‘A Child’s Thought’, Robert Louis Stevenson lets us enter a child’s imagination. At bedtime, the child pictures a magical world. When morning comes, something changes.

“Let’s read a little at a time. As we listen, keep one question in mind: what can the child find in imagination that cannot be found in the room?”

## Scene 2 — Passage 1: Pictures at bedtime

**READ:**

> At seven, when I go to bed,  
> I find such pictures in my head;

**READING DIRECTION:** Warm and expectant. A small breath after “bed”; let “pictures in my head” sound full of possibility. Hold for two seconds.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Preserve the two lines. Highlight `At seven`, then `pictures in my head`. Show a thought bubble opening above the child.

**SAY:**

“The child is speaking directly to us, using the word ‘I’. Seven is bedtime here.

“These ‘pictures’ are scenes in the child’s mind. They are not photographs hanging on the wall! The phrase ‘in my head’ tells us where to look: inside the imagination.

“The word ‘find’ makes these pictures sound like discoveries. The child is about to tell us what this inner world contains.”

## Scene 3 — Passage 2: Beauty, magic, and adventure

**READ:**

> Castles with dragons prowling round,  
> Gardens where magic fruits are found;  
> Fair ladies prisoned in a tower,  
> Or lost in an enchanted bower;

**READING DIRECTION:** Give “dragons” a little suspense and “magic fruits” a sense of wonder. Use the punctuation for light pauses; avoid stopping heavily after every line. Hold for two seconds.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Reveal the four images in reading order. A dragon moves slowly around the castle. Fruit glows gently. A lady is visible in a tower; a separate imagined scene shows a lady in a shaded bower. Present these as possibilities in a storybook landscape.

**SAY:**

“What a busy imagination! We have castles, dragons, magical gardens, and ladies in mysterious places.

“‘Prowling’ means moving quietly and carefully. The dragons seem watchful. The magic fruit adds wonder to the gardens.

“Here, ‘fair’ means beautiful. ‘Prisoned’ means imprisoned, or kept captive. The ladies may be shut in a tower, or lost in an ‘enchanted bower’. A bower is a pleasant shaded place under trees, and ‘enchanted’ means under a magic spell.

“Which details make this world seem different from an ordinary garden?”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds. Keep the passage and pictures visible.]**

**SAY:**

“The dragons, magic fruit, and enchanted bower belong to a world of fantasy. It combines beauty with adventure and a hint of danger. The poem gives us ingredients for an exciting story, without telling us a whole rescue or battle.”

## Scene 4 — Passage 3: The land of dreams

**READ:**

> While gallant horsemen ride by streams  
> That border all this land of dreams  
> I find, so clearly in my head  
> At seven, when I go to bed.

**READING DIRECTION:** Keep “streams / That border…” connected. Read through to the final full stop, with a light breath before “I find”. Give the end of this first stanza a three-second pause.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Riders pass beside streams at the edges of the imagined land. Return to the child’s thought bubble on `so clearly in my head`. Mark the end of **STANZA 1**.

**SAY:**

“‘Gallant’ means brave. The horsemen ride beside streams—small, narrow rivers. These streams ‘border’ the dream land: they run along its edges.

“The final two lines bring us back to the child in bed. The words ‘so clearly’ show how vivid the pictures are. Something can feel very clear in your imagination without being physically in front of you.

“Notice that the stanza ends with the same bedtime line that began it. We have travelled around the dream world and returned to the moment when the child imagines it.”

## Scene 5 — Passage 4: Morning changes the picture

**READ:**

> At seven, when I wake again,  
> The magic land I seek in vain;  
> A chair stands where the castle frowned,  
> The carpet hides the garden ground,

**READING DIRECTION:** Shift gently from wonder to searching and disappointment. Keep the final comma open: the description of the room continues. Hold for two seconds.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Show morning light and the clock at seven. The dream image dissolves into the room. Align the castle with the chair and the garden with the carpet. Introduce **STANZA 2** and the label **ON WAKING**.

**SAY:**

“Now the child wakes. ‘Seek’ means look for, and ‘in vain’ means without success. The child looks for the magical land but cannot find it.

“What occupies the places where the castle and garden seemed to be?”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds. Leave both pairs of images and the lines visible.]**

**SAY:**

“A chair is there instead of the castle, and a carpet covers the place of the garden. These are familiar objects in the waking room. The poem is comparing two ways the child experiences the same surroundings.

“The garden is not really hidden underneath the carpet! The imagined landscape has given way to the ordinary room.

“Keep an eye on ‘the castle frowned’. We will come back to that interesting description.”

## Scene 6 — Passage 5: The ordinary room returns

**READ:**

> No fairies trip across the floor,  
> Boots, and not horsemen, flank the door,  
> And where the blue streams rippling ran  
> Is now a bath and water-can;

**READING DIRECTION:** Gently stress “No” and “not”. Read “ran / Is now…” as one connected thought. Hold for two seconds.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Show the room’s empty floor, boots beside the doorway, and a bath with a water-can. Brief labelled comparisons recall the riders and streams. Fairies appear only as a faded imagined comparison, not as real visitors fleeing the room.

**SAY:**

“There are no fairies moving across the floor. Here, ‘trip’ means move lightly, with quick little steps. It does not mean that the fairies stumble and fall.

“‘Flank’ means be at the side of something. Boots stand beside the door where the child had imagined horsemen.

“‘Rippling’ describes water moving in small waves. The beautiful blue streams are gone from the child’s view. In their place are a bath and a water-can, a container for water.

“The change is striking: a whole flowing landscape has become ordinary things in a room. The repeated negatives—‘No fairies’ and ‘not horsemen’—draw attention to what the child misses.”

## Scene 7 — Passage 6: Looking for the magic

**READ:**

> I seek the magic land in vain  
> At seven, when I wake again.

**READING DIRECTION:** Wistful, with clear emphasis on “in vain”. Allow three seconds after the final line.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** The child looks around the room. The dream landscape remains faintly visible within a thought bubble, while the real room stays ordinary. Do not restore the dream as an event after the ending.

**SAY:**

“Why does the child keep looking for the magic land?”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds. Show ‘What does the child miss?’]**

**SAY:**

“The imagined world was vivid and exciting, and the child wants to find it again. But ‘in vain’ tells us that the search is unsuccessful.

“The child seems disappointed and longs for the dream world. That is an interpretation supported by the repeated search and the contrast with the ordinary room.

“The poem does not say that imagination has disappeared forever. It describes what the child cannot find on waking at this moment.”

## Scene 8 — Put the two worlds side by side

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Build the comparison one row at a time. Keep short phrases, not full paragraphs.

| Bedtime imagination | On waking |
| --- | --- |
| Castle | Chair |
| Garden | Carpet |
| Horsemen | Boots beside the door |
| Blue streams | Bath and water-can |
| Magical figures and movement | No fairies across the floor |

**SAY:**

“Let’s connect the two halves. In the first stanza, the child finds the dream world clearly in the mind. In the second, the child seeks it in the room and cannot find it.

“Castle and chair. Garden and carpet. Horsemen and boots. Streams and a bath with a water-can. These paired details make the contrast easy to picture.

“The poem explores the power of a child’s imagination and the disappointment of returning to ordinary surroundings. The title, ‘A Child’s Thought’, keeps our attention on the child’s inner experience.”

## Scene 9 — How the poet creates the effect

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Three successive cards: **PERSONIFICATION**, **RHYME**, **REPETITION AND CONTRAST**. Highlight exact quotations as they are discussed. Briefly show the poem as two blocks of ten lines.

**SAY:**

“First, personification. In ‘the castle frowned’, the castle receives a human action. People can frown; buildings cannot literally do that. The phrase makes the castle seem stern or forbidding, as though it has a personality.

“Second, listen to the rhyme: ‘bed’ and ‘head’; ‘round’ and ‘found’; ‘tower’ and ‘bower’. These matching sounds give the verse a pleasing musical pattern. We find rhyming pairs in the second stanza too, such as ‘floor’ and ‘door’.

“Third, repetition. ‘At seven’ returns, but bedtime becomes waking time. The first stanza begins and ends with going to bed. The second begins and ends with waking again. Each stanza has ten lines.

“This balanced shape helps us notice what changes: the world the child experiences, and the child’s feelings about it.”

## Scene 10 — Check your understanding

**SCREEN / ACTION:** One question at a time. Reveal the answer after the thinking pause. These are oral practice questions in this first video, not app-enforced checkpoints.

**SAY:**

“Let’s check our understanding. Say your answer aloud, or pause the video if you need more time.

“One. Name two things in the child’s imaginary world. Where does the child find these pictures?”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds.]**

**SAY:**

“You could name castles, dragons, magical gardens, ladies in a tower or bower, horsemen, or streams. The child finds the pictures in the mind at bedtime.

“Two. What figure of speech is used in ‘the castle frowned’? Explain how it works.”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds.]**

**SAY:**

“It is personification. A castle is given the human action of frowning, making it seem stern or forbidding. A complete answer gives the name of the device and explains the words.

“Three. How do the child’s feelings change on waking? Support your answer with a phrase from the poem.”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds.]**

**SAY:**

“At bedtime, the child seems delighted by the vivid magical pictures. On waking, the child seems disappointed and wishes to find that world again. The phrase ‘seek the magic land in vain’ supports this answer: the child looks for it without success.”

## Scene 11 — Carry the idea with you

**SCREEN / ACTION:** A small thought bubble shows the imagined landscape beside the ordinary bedroom. Recap: **Vivid imagination → Waking contrast → Longing**. Finish on the title and poet.

**SAY:**

“Stevenson lets us experience a familiar feeling: an imaginary world can seem wonderfully real, even though it exists in our minds.

“Remember the contrast between bedtime and morning, the ordinary objects that replace the dream scenes, and the child’s unsuccessful search for the magic land.

“For a small creative challenge, choose one ordinary object in your room. What might it become in an imaginary adventure? Keep that picture in your head—and perhaps turn it into a line of your own.”
