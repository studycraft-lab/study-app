# A Little Grain of Gold
## Video plan and tutor script for approval

**Poet:** Rabindranath Tagore  
**Learner:** Class 6, ICSE  
**Draft:** 1 — script and storyboard only; video production awaits approval  
**Proposed length:** approximately 13–15 minutes, including thinking pauses  
**Selected format:** Tutor voice with highlighted poem text and animated illustrations, as requested; English narration, 16:9 landscape, 1080p, with captions.

### How the lesson will work

The tutor reads the entire poem, in eight manageable passages. Each passage stays on screen while the tutor explains its literal meaning, important words, and any deeper meaning. The reading pauses at complete thoughts, rather than at every printed line break. Three short thinking pauses invite the child to predict or interpret before hearing the explanation.

The lesson moves from understanding the story to interpreting its message. By the end, the learner should be able to retell the encounter, explain the speaker's changing feelings, understand the older language, identify the simile and the unexpected reversal of roles, and support an explanation of generosity and regret with words from the poem.

| Part | Approximate duration | What the learner sees and hears |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Welcome and reading key | 1 minute | Title, poet, a simple question, and a quick guide to the old-fashioned words |
| Eight read-and-explain passages | 8–9 minutes | Every word of the poem, read with expression, followed by explanation and restrained animation |
| Deeper meaning and poetic techniques | 2 minutes | King, grain, and gold as symbols; simile, irony, and parable |
| Check understanding and recap | 2–3 minutes | Three questions with thinking time and model answers; the emotional journey and central message |

These are planning estimates. Final scene timings and highlights will follow the recorded narration.

### Screen and sound direction

- Use a calm cream background, dark text, and a soft gold highlight. In reading scenes, give roughly two-thirds of the screen to the poem and one-third to the illustration.
- Display one passage at a time, usually three to five short screen lines. Longer passages can use two successive text cards at natural phrase boundaries. Screen wrapping is for readability; it does not create new poetic line numbers or stanzas.
- Move the highlight phrase by phrase with the narrator. During explanation, retain the passage and highlight only the words being discussed. Distinguish **POEM** from **TUTOR EXPLAINS** with a small label.
- Show concise word meanings near the relevant phrase. Keep narration captions in a separate bottom band, with room reserved so that captions never cover the poem.
- Use a consistent village setting, a travelling speaker carrying a pouch, a golden chariot, a gracious royal figure, and a single grain. Portray the speaker with dignity. Use a general royal figure; explain the spiritual interpretation in words.
- Use movement to explain: the chariot approaches, the king extends his hand, the speaker hesitates, a tiny grain is selected, and the bag is emptied at dusk. Do not reveal the gold before the relevant passage.
- Read the poem a little more slowly than the explanation, with warmth and expression. Use the king's question gently, without a comic or demanding voice. Allow the final line to settle before explaining it.
- Keep the teaching speech clear and foregrounded. Any music should be limited to the opening and closing. No sound effects during important words or thinking pauses.

### Script notation

**READ** contains the poem's words. **SAY** contains the tutor's spoken explanation. **SCREEN / ACTION** and bracketed pauses are production directions and are not spoken.

The passages below retain the printed wording, including “me thought.” The scan's handwritten annotations are not included in the poem text. Passage numbers are navigation aids for this video, not textbook line numbers.

## Scene 1 — Welcome and a reading key

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Title and poet. A village path appears beside a closed pouch. Keep the ending hidden. Briefly introduce the word cards when the tutor names them.

**SAY:**

“Imagine you are hoping someone will help you. But when that person arrives, they ask you for something instead. How would you feel?

“Today, we are reading ‘A Little Grain of Gold’ by Rabindranath Tagore. We will read it a little at a time, picture what happens, and explore what the story means.

“The speaker is the person telling the story. Here, he is a man who survives by asking others for help. When he says ‘I’, we hear this character's voice.

“You will notice some old-fashioned words. ‘Thou’ and ‘thee’ mean ‘you’. ‘Thy’ means ‘your’. Keep those meanings in mind as we begin.”

**SCREEN:** `thou / thee → you` and `thy → your`. Fade these cards before the reading.

## Scene 2 — Passage 1: A wonderful arrival

**READ:**

> I had gone a-begging from door to door in the village path when thy golden chariot appeared in the distance like a gorgeous dream and I wondered who was this king of all kings!

**READING DIRECTION:** A light breath after “village path”; express wonder at “like a gorgeous dream”. Pause for two seconds after the passage.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Highlight in phrase-sized steps. Show the speaker walking past village homes; a golden chariot approaches in the distance. Focus on `a-begging`, `chariot`, and `gorgeous` during the explanation.

**SAY:**

“The speaker has been going from house to house, asking for help. ‘A-begging’ simply means begging. Suddenly, he sees a magnificent golden chariot. A chariot is a vehicle pulled by horses.

“‘Gorgeous’ means very beautiful. The chariot seems so wonderful that he compares it to a beautiful dream. Notice the word ‘like’: it helps us spot a simile, a comparison between two different things.

“The speaker wonders who this ‘king of all kings’ could be. That phrase makes the visitor seem greater than an ordinary king. We will return to its deeper meaning later.”

## Scene 3 — Passage 2: Great expectations

**READ:**

> My hopes rose high and me thought my evil days were at an end, and I stood waiting for alms to be given unasked and for wealth scattered on all sides in the dust.

**READING DIRECTION:** Sound hopeful. Give “unasked” a little emphasis. Pause for two seconds at the end.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** A thought bubble shows the speaker imagining gifts. Mark it clearly as his expectation. Highlight `evil days`, `alms`, and `unasked` in turn.

**SAY:**

“Seeing the king, the speaker hopes his difficult life is about to change. ‘Me thought’ means ‘it seemed to me’. His ‘evil days’ are his days of suffering and hardship. The phrase does not mean that he is an evil person.

“‘Alms’ are gifts, such as food or money, given to people in need. ‘Unasked’ means without his even having to ask.

“He imagines so much wealth that it will be scattered everywhere. This is what he hopes for; it has not happened. At this point, he is thinking about what he will receive.”

## Scene 4 — Passage 3: The king stops

**READ:**

> The chariot stopped where I stood. Thy glance fell on me and thou camest down with a smile. I felt that the luck of my life had come at last.

**READING DIRECTION:** Pause briefly at the full stops. Read “at last” with relief and expectation. Pause for two seconds after the passage.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** The chariot stops beside the speaker. The king steps down and smiles. Small word cards: `glance = a quick look`; `thou camest = you came`.

**SAY:**

“The chariot stops exactly where the speaker is standing. The king looks at him, comes down, and smiles.

“‘Thy glance fell on me’ means ‘you looked at me’. Nothing literally falls! ‘Thou camest down’ means ‘you came down’.

“The king's friendly attention makes the speaker even more hopeful. He feels that his great moment of good fortune has finally arrived. So far, everything seems to support his expectation of receiving a gift.”

## Scene 5 — Passage 4: An unexpected question

**READ:**

> Then of a sudden thou didst hold out thy right hand and say “What hast thou to give to me?”

**READING DIRECTION:** Read the question gently and distinctly. Hold for three seconds afterwards.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** The king extends an empty, open hand. Display the question prominently. Below it, reveal the modern wording during explanation: `What do you have to give me?`

**SAY:**

“Suddenly, the king holds out his hand. ‘Thou didst’ means ‘you did’, and ‘hast’ means ‘have’. His question means, ‘What do you have to give me?’

“Stop and think: why is this so surprising?”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds. Show ‘Who expected to receive a gift?’]**

**SAY:**

“The speaker expected the rich king to give something to him. Instead, the king asks him to give. Their expected roles have been reversed. This surprising contrast is an example of irony.”

## Scene 6 — Passage 5: Confusion and hesitation

**READ:**

> Ah, what a kingly jest was it to open thy palm to a beggar to beg! I was confused and stood undecided,

**READING DIRECTION:** Express bewilderment, without making the speaker sound foolish. This passage ends at a comma: use a short pause, not the final tone of a completed sentence.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** The speaker looks from the king's open hand to his own pouch. Highlight `kingly jest` and `undecided`. The scene briefly holds during explanation.

**SAY:**

“A ‘jest’ is a joke. ‘Kingly’ means royal, or belonging to a king. The speaker thinks this must be a strange royal joke: a king asking a beggar for something!

“That is the speaker's reaction. The poem does not say that the king is laughing at him.

“‘Undecided’ means he cannot make up his mind. He pauses, unsure what to do. His hope has changed into confusion. The sentence continues with the choice he finally makes.”

## Scene 7 — Passage 6: The smallest gift

**READ:**

> and then from my wallet I slowly took out the least little grain of corn and gave it to thee.

**READING DIRECTION:** Slow slightly on “slowly” and “least little grain”. Pause for two seconds afterwards.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Show a small cloth pouch containing grain. The speaker hesitates, selects one tiny grain, and places it in the king's hand. Enlarge the grain in a small inset so its size can be understood.

**SAY:**

“Here, ‘wallet’ means a small bag or pouch. ‘Corn’ refers to grain. He takes out the smallest little grain and gives it to the king.

“Which words tell us that he is reluctant to give?”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds. Keep the passage visible.]**

**SAY:**

“‘Slowly’ suggests hesitation. ‘Least little’ stresses how tiny his gift is. He does give something, but he holds back almost everything.

“We can understand that someone with very little may worry about losing it. The poem asks us to notice this particular choice: he hopes to receive generously, but finds it difficult to give generously.”

## Scene 8 — Passage 7: A discovery at day's end

**READ:**

> But how great my surprise when at the day’s end I emptied my bag on the floor to find a least little grain of gold among the poor heap.

**READING DIRECTION:** Give “But” a slight lift; express quiet amazement at “gold”. Pause for three seconds after the passage.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Shift to dusk. The speaker empties his bag. Reveal one tiny golden grain among the modest contents. Then compare the grain he gave with the gold he finds, using equal-size close-ups labelled `Given` and `Found`.

**SAY:**

“At the end of the day, the speaker empties his bag and discovers a tiny grain of gold among his few possessions. That small collection is the ‘poor heap’.

“Remember ‘the least little grain of corn’? Now we hear ‘a least little grain of gold’. The repeated words connect his tiny gift with the precious thing he finds.

“The poem suggests that what he gave has returned to him in a more precious form. We are told about his discovery; we are not shown the moment of any transformation.”

**PRODUCTION NOTE:** Do not animate the king visibly changing corn into gold as though the poem describes that event. The visual reveal occurs when the bag is emptied.

## Scene 9 — Passage 8: Regret

**READ:**

> I bitterly wept and wished that I had the heart to give thee my all.

**READING DIRECTION:** Read quietly, with sadness. Stress “had the heart” gently. Hold the final line for four seconds.

**SCREEN / ACTION:** The speaker holds the golden grain and looks thoughtful and tearful. Keep the final line large and still.

**SAY:**

“‘Bitterly wept’ means cried with deep sorrow. ‘Had the heart’ means had the willingness or generosity. ‘My all’ means everything I had.

“Why does he feel sad after finding something valuable?”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds.]**

**SAY:**

“He realises that he held back. On the story's surface, he may wish he had given more because even his tiny gift brought something precious. More deeply, he regrets missing the chance to give wholeheartedly.

“Notice what he wishes for: the heart to give. His regret concerns his attitude as well as the size of his gift. The ending leaves us thinking about the opportunity he has missed.”

## Scene 10 — What might the story mean?

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Show three simple picture cards: king, grain, gold. Add one meaning at a time, under the label `A deeper reading`.

**SAY:**

“The textbook invites us to read this poem as a parable. A parable is a simple story that teaches a moral or spiritual lesson.

“In this spiritual reading, the ‘king of all kings’ represents God. The speaker can represent a human being who asks for blessings but struggles to give. The king's request can be understood as an invitation to generosity and trust.

“A symbol is something in a story that also suggests a bigger idea. The small grain can represent what we are willing to offer. The gold can represent the precious value of giving, including spiritual richness.

“Does this mean that giving a pencil will make a gold pencil appear? No. This is a story with a deeper meaning, not a promise of material rewards. Its lesson is to give sincerely, without treating kindness as a bargain.

“For us, generosity can include sharing our time, helping someone understand a lesson, or offering something we can spare. The poem asks us to think about the willingness behind the gift.”

## Scene 11 — The poet's choices

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Reveal three cards in sequence. Each includes the exact short quotation and one simple label. Do not display all explanations at once.

**SAY:**

“Let's notice three choices that make the poem effective.

“First, the simile ‘like a gorgeous dream’ compares the golden chariot to a wonderful dream, using ‘like’. It helps us imagine the speaker's wonder.

“Second, the king asks the beggar for a gift. This reversal of what we expect creates irony and makes us stop and think.

“Third, the words ‘least little grain’ connect the small gift with the small piece of gold. Their repetition helps us understand the speaker's regret.

“The title brings us back to that tiny piece of gold. It is small in size, but it leads to a big discovery about giving.”

## Scene 12 — Check your understanding

**SCREEN / ACTION:** One question at a time. Show a small five-second thinking indicator. Reveal each answer only when the tutor begins the answer. Caption answers as concise keywords, not full paragraphs.

**SAY:**

“Let's check our understanding. Say your answer aloud, or pause the video if you need more time.

“One: where did the chariot stop, and who came down?”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds.]**

**SAY:**

“It stopped where the speaker was standing, and the king came down with a smile.

“Two: explain ‘kingly jest’. Why did the speaker think the request was a joke?”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds.]**

**SAY:**

“It means a royal joke. He was confused because he expected the wealthy king to give him something, but the king asked him for a gift instead.

“Three: why was the speaker both surprised and sorrowful at the end?”

**[THINKING PAUSE: 5 seconds.]**

**SAY:**

“He was surprised to find a tiny grain of gold. He was sorrowful because he had given so little and wished he had been generous enough to give his all.

“When you write an answer, explain the idea and support it with a phrase from the poem. For the final question, ‘the least little grain’ and ‘had the heart to give’ are useful evidence.”

## Scene 13 — A final look back

**SCREEN / ACTION:** Reveal the emotional journey in five steps: `Hope → Confusion → Reluctance → Surprise → Regret`. End with the title, poet, and a short takeaway: `Give with a willing heart.`

**SAY:**

“Follow the speaker's feelings one last time. He hopes to receive. He is confused by the king's request. He gives reluctantly, discovers the gold with surprise, and finally feels regret.

“Tagore uses this small encounter to ask a large question: when we have a chance to give, how willing is our heart?

“Think of one kind thing you could do today without expecting a reward. That is a useful way to carry the poem's message into your own life.”

**[END HOLD: 3 seconds.]**
