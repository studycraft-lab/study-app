- **Format:** An illustrated, captioned video with spoken questions, seven-second silent thinking pauses, and answers. Pause the video for longer when needed. Narration is generated offline, with no paid voice service.
**Scope:** The story and its literary vocabulary. The textbook's transitive/intransitive verb lesson is separate.

## Teaching guardrails

- The boy and man are father and son. They sit in a still boat on a wilderness lake in late afternoon, waiting for a flock of teal. The father is the man with a gun; the boy is also armed.
- The boy loves watching birds. His father's firing misses the high flock. The boy patiently aims one shot at a bird leading a formation and wounds it. Another bird takes its place. Do not imply that the whole flock falls or that the duck dies.
- The duck has a broken wing, is alive, and cannot fly again. The boy is frightened and relieved when it lifts its head. He takes it home, bandages the wing, feeds it and watches it in the backyard.
- The boy throws away his gun. The father is shocked at first, then feels relief and peace. The story does not say that the duck recovers its power of flight or that the father makes an explicit promise about hunting.
- Explain **teal**, **formation**, **spellbound**, **plummeted**, **bulrushes**, **ragged**, **maimed**, **lopsided**, and **defiantly** in the story's context. Distinguish what the text states from an inference about the characters' feelings.
- Show the shot without a graphic impact. The video should draw attention to the bird's vulnerability and the boy's change of heart, without turning the hunt into an exciting game.

## Scene 1 — A lake, a father, a son

**VISUAL:** A still lake at late afternoon, the red volcanic hill, and a small boat with its oars tucked in. Title and author. Keep the birds distant.

**SAY:** Welcome to *Wild Duck*, a story by Romesh Gunesekera. You have read it already; now let us retrace what happens and why the ending matters. A father and son sit in a motionless boat on a wilderness lake. They both have guns. The father points towards a red volcanic hill, where he expects the wild ducks to appear. The boy has come to shoot, but watching birds is his passion. Keep that difference in mind: at first, what interests the boy most—the gun, or the living flock?

**ASK:** Pause and say it aloud: where are the father and son, and what are they waiting for?

**REVEAL:** They are in a small boat on a lake, waiting for wild ducks—teal—to fly over. The oars are tucked in, so the boat stays still.

## Scene 2 — The sky changes

**VISUAL:** The water reflects orange, red and mauve. A ribbon of dots grows into flocks of teal that bank and form patterns across half the sky.

**SAY:** Light changes across the flat water: orange, red and mauve seep from the sky. It is late afternoon. Suddenly the boy sees a ribbon of black dots approaching. He is spellbound—so fascinated that he almost forgets to breathe. Huge numbers of teal fill half the sky. They bank, or tilt and turn, and smaller groups dive and rise in formations. A formation is an organised pattern made by birds flying together. Notice how the scene invites us to admire the flock before anyone shoots.

**ASK:** What does *spellbound* tell you about the boy's reaction to the birds?

**REVEAL:** He is captivated and full of wonder. Birdwatching is his passion; the flock is more than a target to him.

## Scene 3 — Two ways of looking

**VISUAL:** Split the screen: the father watches through his gun sight while the boy watches the changing shapes of the flock. Keep the guns small within the wider lake scene.

**SAY:** The father tells his son to be quick, aim ahead of the birds, and manage with one shot. When the teal pass overhead, the father fires repeatedly. The explosions shake the boat and ripple the lake, but he says the birds are flying too high. The boy's movements are slower and steadier. He picks one bird out of the great mass, a leader in a formation, and fires. The bird plummets: it drops sharply towards the water. Another bird immediately takes its place in the formation. The father cheers, but the boy wants to find the fallen duck.

**ASK:** Who hits a bird, and what happens to the flock when that bird falls?

**REVEAL:** The boy hits one bird. It falls, but another bird takes its place and the flock continues flying. The father fired too, but the story does not say that he hit a bird.

## Scene 4 — Finding the duck

**VISUAL:** The boat approaches bulrushes at the water's edge. Show a small duck floating with one injured wing, without blood or a graphic wound.

**SAY:** The father directs the boy towards the bulrushes, the tall reeds near the water. The boy paddles against the tide. His arms tremble. He sees a shape floating there: the duck has a broken wing, described as ragged, like the torn sail of a toy boat. He scoops it up. The father says, “Well done.” Yet the boy is silent. Touching the soft feathers and the thin, bony neck makes the duck real to him in a new way. He looks frightened by what he has done.

**ASK:** Why does “Well done” fail to make the boy happy?

**REVEAL:** He sees and touches the wounded bird. His attention shifts from making a successful shot to the suffering of a living creature.

## Scene 5 — Alive, but injured

**VISUAL:** Close, gentle view of the duck lifting its head in the boy's hands. The boy strokes it; the boat turns towards home.

**SAY:** Then the duck lifts its head. It is not dead. The boy breathes in relief and strokes it. He says he wants to go home. The father tells him he will have to look after the bird, and the boy answers that he wants to. This is the turning point. Relief does not erase what happened: the wing is broken. But the boy now chooses responsibility for the duck he injured.

**ASK:** What two feelings can the boy have at once when the duck lifts its head?

**REVEAL:** He can feel relieved that the duck is alive and upset or guilty that he wounded it. Both feelings fit his actions in the story.

## Scene 6 — Care at home

**VISUAL:** A quiet backyard. The boy bandages the duck's wing, offers food and watches it waddle. Show a gentle attempt to flap its uneven wings; do not show it flying.

**SAY:** At home, the boy bandages the wing and learns how to feed the maimed duck. *Maimed* means seriously injured. He spends hours watching it waddle around the backyard. Sometimes it stretches its neck and tries to flap its lopsided wings. *Lopsided* means uneven, with one side different from the other. The text is clear about the lasting result: the bird can never fly again. Caring for it does not undo the injury, but it shows how deeply the boy has changed.

**ASK:** What does the boy do for the duck, and what can the duck no longer do?

**REVEAL:** He bandages its wing, feeds it and looks after it. Because its wing is broken, it can never fly again.

## Scene 7 — The missing gun

**VISUAL:** At home, the father notices the gun is absent. Show the boy standing calmly beside the duck, then a quiet expression on the father's face. Do not show anyone firing.

**SAY:** One day the father asks where the boy's gun is. The boy says he threw it away, defiantly—boldly, even though he expects his father to object. The father is shocked. The boy insists that the duck is his to care for. The father argues that the gun gave him the duck; the boy replies that using it made him feel sick. He cannot separate that object from the harm he caused. Then the father's anger gives way to relief and a sense of peace. The story lets us see a change in both of them.

**ASK:** Why does the boy throw away his gun? Answer with a feeling and a reason from the story.

**REVEAL:** He feels sick about injuring the duck and no longer wants the gun that caused its suffering. Looking after the duck has deepened his compassion.

## Scene 8 — Remember the language

**VISUAL:** Six clear word cards around a lake illustration. Each card appears with a tiny visual cue: flock pattern, falling dot, reeds, torn wing, uneven wings, and firm stance.

**SAY:** Let us collect the words that carry the story. *Spellbound*: completely fascinated by the flock. *Formation*: the pattern of birds flying together. *Plummeted*: fell quickly from the sky. *Bulrushes*: tall plants growing by the water. *Ragged*: torn and uneven, like the injured wing. *Maimed*: seriously injured. *Lopsided*: uneven. *Defiantly*: standing by a decision despite opposition. Try saying two of these words in your own sentence about the boy and the duck.

**ASK:** Which word means “fell quickly,” and which describes the duck's uneven wings?

**REVEAL:** *Plummeted* means fell quickly. *Lopsided* describes the uneven wings. *Ragged* describes the torn-looking injured wing.

## Scene 9 — Tell the story back

**VISUAL:** A simple five-step path: still boat → flock → falling bird → care at home → gun gone. Leave the last step blank until the answer.

**SAY:** Now tell the story back in five steps. First, the father and son wait on the lake. Second, the boy watches the remarkable flock. Third, he shoots one duck. Fourth, he discovers it is alive but badly injured and cares for it at home. Fifth, he throws away his gun. The sequence matters because his decision at the end grows from what he saw, touched and did after the shot.

**ASK:** Pause for as long as you need. What is the moment when the boy begins to think differently about shooting?

**REVEAL:** Finding and touching the helpless, injured duck changes his view. Its lifting its head brings relief, and caring for it strengthens that change.

## Scene 10 — One last thought

**VISUAL:** Return to the lake at dusk. The flock is distant; the duck remains safely in the backyard in a small inset. End with three takeaways, not a moral slogan.

**SAY:** Here are three ideas to remember. The boy begins as someone who loves watching birds, yet he is there to shoot. Seeing the wounded duck turns a distant bird into a creature he feels responsible for. He cares for it and rejects his gun, while his father is relieved rather than angry in the end. *Wild Duck* asks us to notice how direct experience can change a person's choices. When you revise, use precise details—the moving formation, the broken wing and the missing gun—to support that idea.

**ASK:** Final quick question: what detail best shows that the father has changed too?

**REVEAL:** After learning the gun is gone, he feels relief and peace. The text shows that reaction, though it does not tell us what he will do in the future.

**AFTER:** You have finished the revision. If one answer felt difficult, revisit that scene or the corresponding page in the chapter. Then try telling the whole story in your own words, from the still boat to the father's feeling of peace.
